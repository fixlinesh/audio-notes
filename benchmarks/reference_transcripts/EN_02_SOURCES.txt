To analyze the war through original historical documents, we compare two primary sources written between 1941 and 1943 that reflect the two sides of our topic, the state's voice of industrial evacuation and personal voice of the Kazakh soldier at the front. Primary source 1 consists of the official 1941 to 1942 decrees of the Council for Evacuation and the Sovnarkom of the Kazakh SSR on relocating factories and cultural institutions. Written in strict bureaucratic language, this document ordered local authorities in Alma-Ata, Karaganda, and Shymkent to receive, house, and restart 220 evacuated factories within weeks. This source shows how the Soviet state viewed Kazakhstan primarily as a secure resource base rich in coal, copper, and lead. Primary source 2 is the famous letter from the Kazakh people to frontline soldiers published in 1943, alongside frontline letters written home by Kazakh soldiers such as Bauyrzhan Momyshuly and Aliya Moldagulova. Unlike the dry economic decrees, these letters use traditional Kazakh poetic imagery invoking the spirit of ancestors, family honor, and the protection of the native steppe to inspire soldiers in battle. Comparing these two sources reveals a crucial historical insight: while state decrees treated mobilization as a top-down logistical plan, personal letters show that ordinary Kazakhs connected the war effort to their own cultural values and survival. Right there in museum display case we see authentic wartime folded letters, military ID cards, and frontline newspapers. These fragile paper artifacts survived the trenches and serve as direct material evidence of the emotional bond between soldiers at the front and their families working in the rear.
